Attic Water Damage in Richardson

Attic water damage comes from two directions. From above, rain enters through failed flashing, worn shingles, or the seals at chimneys and vents. The house itself sends moisture upward too, and in a poorly ventilated attic it condenses on cold sheathing. Either way, hardly anyone goes up there to look. For Richardson homeowners, our crew examines the attic, identifies where the water comes from, and dries the space.

On the typical attic job, soaked insulation is the main issue. Once wet, both cellulose and fiberglass lose R-value, and heating and cooling the home costs more. The bigger problem is that for weeks, saturated insulation holds moisture on the drywall of the ceiling and on the framing. Our process is to pull the saturated insulation, dry the wood using dehumidifiers and air movers, and treat mold found on rafters or sheathing.

What It Looks Like

  • Ceilings upstairs with brownish stains or rings that grow following storms
  • Rafters and roof decking marked with dark streaks or stains
  • Water dripping from, or staining around, a ceiling light or bathroom fan
  • Light from outside visible along a roof valley or around a chimney or vent pipe
  • Rusty nail tips poking through the sheathing, sometimes with water beads on them
  • Insulation that looks matted, flattened, or darker than the insulation around it
What We Do

Our Attic Water Damage Process

1

Inspect and Find the Source

We check the roof deck, flashing points, vents and exhaust ducts to learn whether the water is a leak, condensation, or both. Its full reach is mapped with a thermal camera and meter readings.

2

Take Out Wet Insulation

We bag and remove insulation that is moldy or saturated, which lets the drywall and wood beneath it dry. We protect the living space below and keep the access path contained.

3

Dry the Framing

Air movers and dehumidifiers bring the rafters, joists and sheathing back to normal moisture levels. An antimicrobial goes on after any visible mold has been cleaned from the wood.

4

Correct the Source

The roof or ventilation issue behind it is pointed out to you, and we are glad to pass what we found to your roofer. Once the attic stays dry, new insulation goes in to restore the R-value.

What You Cannot See

What Hides Behind It

Lost Insulation Value

Compressed, damp insulation does very little insulating, and it may not recover after it dries. Rooms under the wet section run hotter or colder, and energy bills rise.

Mold on Sheathing

With poor ventilation, mold commonly grows on the bottom of the roof deck. A wide area may be covered by the time somebody climbs up to check.

Bath Fans Venting Inside

Every day, a fan duct that stops short of the roof releases humid bathroom air into the attic. That moisture condenses on the cold sheathing and looks just like a roof leak.

Leaks From Attic Equipment

In a lot of homes the attic houses an air handler along with its condensate line and pan. A clogged drain or an overflowing pan drips into the insulation for weeks, with no storm to warn you.

The Clock

What Happens if You Wait

The first storm

Insulation Soaks It Up

Rain that gets past the roof lands in the insulation, which absorbs it. With no sign inside the house, nobody notices the leak and the roof stays unrepaired.

Hours 24 to 48

Mold May Begin

Damp wood and paper facing in a warm, still attic are ideal for mold. Growth can begin on the sheathing and on the back of the ceiling drywall.

Weeks later

Stains Come Through

Once storm after storm has saturated the insulation, the ceiling starts getting wet. The drywall may sag, and rings of brown staining appear on the ceiling of the room below.

Months later

Wood Starts Rotting

The sheathing comes apart in layers and no longer grips the roofing nails. Rafters stay damp enough to decay, and mold covers large areas of the roof deck.
